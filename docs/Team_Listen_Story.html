Does the Team Actually Listen?

The whole project told as a picture story — the problem, why it matters, how our machine works, and what we expect to find.

1The problem: robots that nod along

You tell a pair of warehouse robots what to do. They do something sensible, the boxes get moved, everyone is happy. But here is the uncomfortable question researchers started asking single robots in 2025 — and nobody has asked teams:

Scene 1 — the same successful episode, seen from outside and from inside. Success rates cannot distinguish obedience from coincidence.
The problem in one sentence: every multi-robot language system today is graded on whether the task got done — never on whether the words mattered.

2Why teams make it urgent

For a single arm, ignoring the words is embarrassing. For a team, the words carry information that cannot be recovered from looking at the scene — three everyday situations show it:

Scene 2 — role binding, precedence, exclusion: instructions a camera cannot substitute for. Exactly these are missing from every single-arm audit.

And the field is racing ahead: language-commanded robot teams are now appearing in papers monthly — all evaluated by task success alone. If their language channels are dead, we are building fleets that only seem commandable. That is a safety problem wearing a convenience costume.

3How: the machine we built

Everything runs in simulation on one GPU. Four components, drawn as they connect:

Scene 3 — the component diagram. Only the orange brain is ever trained; the words stay frozen, and the purple bench never touches either — it only watches.

4How: the certificate — proving the words are needed

Before accusing any robot of not listening, we must prove the task requires listening. Our trick: train a spy with perfect vision but no ears.

Scene 4 — the language-necessity certificate. If the earless spy beats the coin, the task leaked and we redesign it; the stamp is earned, never assumed. A second instrument (a “leak probe”) double-checks by trying to read the instruction out of what the robots see.

5How: the twin-world test — catching deafness in the act

Now the core experiment. We clone the world — same maze, same crates, same starting spots, even the same dice rolls — and change exactly one thing: the sentence.

Scene 5 — the twin-world (counterfactual) test. Because even the dice are copied, any behaviour difference can only have come from the words. The CSI meter turns this into a number by comparing the word-swap effect against ordinary luck.

6What we expect to find

Our pre-registered bet is that today’s teams are half-listeners — and the two halves split in a very specific, very teachable way:

Scene 6 — the predicted dissociation. Both teams score near-perfect task success; only the twin-world test exposes that the right team never heard the ordering instruction at all.
If the bet lands: the paper delivers the first proof that a robot team can obey half its orders and ignore the rest while acing every benchmark — plus the certified tasks and the listening meter any future paper can use to check theirs. If the bet fails (teams listen everywhere, or nowhere): the instruments still stand, the pre-registration turns the surprise into an honest headline, and the comparison of the four language-feeding designs becomes the story. Either world produces the paper.

And after the paper: the same bench becomes the diagnostic for the follow-up everyone will want — not just measuring whether teams listen, but training them until they do.
